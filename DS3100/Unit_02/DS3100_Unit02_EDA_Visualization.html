Two concomitant lines of thought

John Tukey — Exploratory Data Analysis

Use data to look, question, compare, and discover.

  • What is unusual?
  • What varies?
  • What relationships deserve attention?

Edward Tufte — Data Visualization

  • Avoid distortions.
  • Make comparison easy.
  • Remove unnecessary decoration.

Tukey, Exploratory Data Analysis (1977); Tufte, The Visual Display of Quantitative Information (1983).

What is EDA?

Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) is a way of using summaries and visualizations to understand the data we have and decide what is worth investigating.

For example, recall the clinic dataset containing data on fictional outpatient visits. It included each patient’s clinic, age, waiting time, and visit status. Some questions that the dataset initially reveal:

  • What does the distribution of waiting times look like?
  • Are there unusually long waits?
  • Do Cardiology and Primary Care appear to have different waiting times?
  • Does waiting time appear to vary with age?
  • Are there patterns we did not expect?

EDA is about discovering and describing patterns — not deciding whether those patterns are statistically significant or causal.

Tip

EDA reveals: what is going on in our observed data. Our central question beyond that remains what patterns or unusual features deserve further investigation.

EDA vs. Confirmatory Data Analysis

Confirmatory Data Analysis (CDA) uses pre-specified questions, statistical models, and inference to evaluate whether the evidence supports a particular claim or hypothesis and to quantify uncertainty.

The distinction becomes clearer when we flesh out the questions we ask at two different stages.

Exploratory Confirmatory / Inferential
In our clinic data, Cardiology patients appear to wait longer than Primary Care patients. Is there evidence that average waiting times differ between Cardiology and Primary Care patients?
How large is the difference we observe? What is the estimated difference in the population?
Are there unusual patients or clinics that might be driving the pattern? How uncertain is our estimate?
Does the relationship between age and blood pressure look roughly linear, nonlinear, or absent? Is age associated with blood pressure in the population?
What patterns deserve further investigation? What conclusions are supported by the analysis, and how uncertain are they?

Note

A useful progression:

Explore → formulate a question → analyze → quantify uncertainty

EDA can motivate a formal analysis, but the exploratory pattern itself is not the inference.

For example, a plot may suggest that Cardiology patients wait longer. A formal analysis might estimate the difference and report a confidence interval to quantify uncertainty around that estimate.

The EDA loop

question
   ↓
inspect
   ↓
clean / transform
   ↓
summarize
   ↓
visualize
   ↓
notice something
   ↓
ask a better question
   ↺

EDA is iterative, not a checklist that ends after a certain set of plots. Instead, we go back and forth to refine our argument.

Start with the data, not the plot

Before making a visualization:

  1. Identify the unit of observation.
  2. Understand variable types and ranges.
  3. Check missingness and suspicious values.
  4. Decide what observations are relevant to the question.
  5. Identify the comparison or relationship you want to see.

A plot cannot rescue a poorly understood dataset.

Let your question choose your viz

What do you want to see? Useful starting point
Distribution of one numeric variable Histogram / density / boxplot
Counts of categories Bar chart
Numeric variable across groups Grouped boxplot / density
Relationship between two numeric variables Scatterplot
Same relationship across groups Color / shape / facets
Many numeric variables Scatterplot matrix

There is no universally “best” plot. The question determines the job of the graphic.

Important

Good visualization is not decoration added after analysis. It is part of how we reason about the data and communicate what we learned.

Tools for Data Visualization

GGPlot and Matplotlib

Grammar of Graphics

The Grammar of Graphics is a formal rule-based framework proposed by Leland Wilkinson.

Think of it as a way to describe statistical graphics as combinations of independent components. ggplot2 adapts this idea into a practical layered system. (Wilkinson, 2005; ggplot2 book)

What we plot?

Data
→ the observations we want to visualize

Visual encodings / aesthetics
→ how variables are represented visually

Examples: position → x- and y-axis; color; size; shape

Geometric objects
→ the marks used to represent the data

Examples: points; bars; lines; boxes

How do we organize graphic?

Scales
→ translate data values into visual properties and define how they are displayed

Statistics
→ calculate or summarize what is shown

Facets
→ split the data into separate panels

Coordinates
→ determine how the spatial system is arranged

Theme / annotations
→ control appearance and add explanatory elements

A useful mental model

Data → Visual encodings → Geometric marks → Scales / statistics → Facets / coordinates → Final graphic

ggplot2: Read grammar, not syntax

We make a plot by specifying what data to use, what to show on your plot and how to display it.

ggplot(
  ten,
  aes(
    x = percent_below,
    y = percent_on_mastered,
    color = overall_subject
  )
) +
  geom_point()

What’s going on here?

Start with the Tennessee data.

  • Put percent_below on the x-axis.**

  • Put percent_on_mastered on the y-axis.**

  • Map overall_subject to color.**

  • Draw the observations as points.**

This type of visual lens is language agnostic and can apply to libraries like Altair as well.

Anatomy of a plot

Component What it does Example
Data supplies observations data = ten
Aesthetics Maps variables to visual properties
: position, color, size, shape, etc.		 aes(x, y, color)
Geometries Geometric objects on your plot to represent data geom_point()
Scales How values are mapped into axes, colors, sizes etc. scale_x_*()
Statistics Transformations or summarizes stat_*() / geom_smooth()
Facets Multi-view plots with smaller panels
each focussing on different subsets of data		 facet_wrap()
Coordinates How the plot’s coordinate system is arranged coord_flip()
Theme The visual appearance: fonts, grids, spacing, titles, etc. theme()

The same idea in Python

Python has several graphics libraries rather than one single dominant grammar.

  • matplotlib — low-level figure and axes control
  • seaborn — concise statistical graphics
  • plotnine/Altair — implementations inspired directly by the Grammar of Graphics

The conceptual structure remains:

data → mappings → marks → scales/layout → annotations

R and Python: different syntax, same questions

Analytical question R / ggplot2 Python / seaborn
What data? ggplot(data=...) data=...
What goes on x/y? aes(x=..., y=...) x=..., y=...
What marks? geom_*() plot function / kind=
What group? color, fill, facets hue, style, facets
What layout? facet_*() FacetGrid, row, col
What styling? theme() / scales matplotlib / seaborn options

The API changes; the analytical decisions do not.

Univariate Visualization

One variable at a time

First thoughts

When we examine one variable at a time, our first goal is to understand how its values are distributed.

For example, for a patient’s waiting time:

  • Where are most observations?
  • How much do the values vary?
  • Is the distribution symmetric or skewed?
  • Are there gaps or multiple clusters?
  • Are there unusually large or small observations?

First question

What does the distribution of this variable look like?

To answer this, we must know what kind of plot is required. A histogram, density plot, or boxplot would probably be our visual starting point.

Histogram

A histogram groups numeric values into evenly sized bins and counts observations in each bin.

R / ggplot2

ten_plot <- ten %>%
  filter(!is.na(percent_below))

p_ggplot <- ggplot(
  ten_plot,
  aes(x = percent_below)
) +
  geom_histogram(
    bins = 15,
    boundary = min(ten_plot$percent_below),,
    fill = "blue"
  )

Python / matplotlib.pyplot

ten = pd.read_csv("data/tenn2018.csv")
plt.hist(
    ten["percent_below"].dropna(),
    bins=15,
    color="blue",
)
plt.xlabel("Percent Below")
plt.ylabel("Frequency")
plt.show()

Histograms: the binning problem

A reasonable bin width is key to capturing the overall shape of the distribution.

  • Broad bins can hide important structure.
  • Very narrow bins can make random variation look like meaningful structure.

Tip

EDA question: How sensitive is the apparent shape of the distribution to the choice of bin width?

The binning should help the reader see meaningful structure without manufacturing patterns. There is no single universally correct number of bins.
Try several reasonable bin widths before deciding what the distribution looks like.

Histogram : Extreme Values

Suppose we have some data on students in a class that contains their GPA. Our GPA variable is expected to range from 0 to 4.

R / ggplot2

load("./Unit_02/data/PluckData.RData")
hist(
  dataset$GPA,
  breaks = 10,
  main = "GPA with an improbable value",
  xlab = "GPA"
)

Python/Matplotlib

plt.hist(
    dataset["GPA"].dropna(),
    bins=10
)

plt.title("GPA with an improbable value")
plt.xlabel("GPA")
plt.ylabel("Frequency")
plt.show()

What went wrong?

The histogram uses the full observed range, from about 2 to 999. Almost all legitimate GPAs are squeezed into the first bin, while the value 999, which is probably an encoding for a missing value, occupies the far end of the range.

The problem is not the number of bins but that an implausible value has distorted the range being binned.

EDA lesson

An unexpected visualization should prompt us to inspect the underlying data before interpreting the distribution. Here you can eliminate all values outside of the expected range (0,4) and encode them as missing/NA/NaN to filter them out.

Density plot

A density plot gives a smoothed, continuous representation of a numeric distribution via kernel density estimation. It attempts to show the probability density of the data

Useful when comparing overlapping distributions.

R

ten_plot <- ten %>%
  filter(!is.na(percent_below))

ggplot(
  ten_plot,
  aes(x = percent_below)
) +
  geom_density()

Python

from scipy.stats import gaussian_kde

x = ten["percent_below"].dropna()
kde = gaussian_kde(x)
x_grid = np.linspace(x.min(), x.max(), 200)

plt.plot(x_grid, kde(x_grid))
plt.xlabel("Percent Below")
plt.ylabel("Density")

plt.show()

Note

Smoothing is itself a choice. A density plot is not the raw data; it is a representation of the data’s distribution.

What are we controlling?

  1. color controls the outline of the density curve.

  2. fill controls the area underneath the curve.

  3. alpha controls the transparency of the fill.

  4. my_theme stores reusable plot-wide styling choices such as fonts of axes, legends and title appearance.

In ggplot2, parts of a plot can be stored as objects and reused:

plot1 + my_theme
plot2 + my_theme
plot3 + my_theme

Here, color and fill are fixed properties of the plot because we assign them specific values outside aes(). If instead we place color or fill inside aes(), they become aesthetic mappings.
ggplot uses color to visually encode the values of a variable, such as different categories (more on this later).

Boxplot

A boxplot is especially useful for comparing distributions across groups. It summarizes a numeric distribution using:

1. Median

2. First and third quartiles

3. Interquartile range (IQR)

4. Potential outliers

R

ten_plot <- ten %>%filter(!is.na(percent_below))
ggplot(
  ten_plot,
  aes(
    x = "",
    y = percent_below
  )
) +
  geom_boxplot(
    width = 0.4,
    color = "black",
    fill = "lightblue"
  )+
  labs(
    title = "Distribution of Percent Below",
    x = NULL,
    y = "Percent Below"
  )

Python

plt.figure(figsize=(4, 6))
plt.boxplot(
    ten_plot,
    vert=True,
    widths=0.4,
    # Box
    boxprops={
        "facecolor": "white",
        "edgecolor": "black"
    },
    # Median
    medianprops={
        "color": "orange"
    },
)

Reading a boxplot

        ─────── upper whisker
        │
     ┌───────┐
     │       │  Q3
     ├───────┤  median
     │       │  Q1
     └───────┘
        │
        ─────── lower whisker

        •  possible outlier

Note

The upper and lower whiskers typically extend from the upper and lower quartiles of the data to the maximum and minimum points, provided they fall within a “reasonable” range. Point beyond that show up as separate dots and are called “outliers”.

The exact definition of “outlier” depends on the plotting convention; in a standard Tukey boxplot, points beyond 1.5 × IQR are flagged.

Reading distribution shape

Histograms and Boxplots are key to understanding not just the standout summary numbers but also the shape of a distribution and where most of the data points are gathered.

Skewness

Checks if one tail longer than the other?

Negative / left skew: longer tail toward smaller values; \(mean < median\)

Approximately symmetric: left and right sides are broadly balanced; mean and median are roughly similar

Positive / right skew: longer tail toward larger values; \(mean > median\)

Kurtosis

Kurtosis describes the relative heaviness of the tails and concentration around the center of a distribution.

  • Higher kurtosis → relatively heavier tails and more extreme observations
  • Lower kurtosis → relatively light, thin tails and fewer extreme outliers

Takeaways

In a histogram, look for a longer tail on one side.

In a boxplot, compare the lengths of the whiskers and the position of the median within the box.

A histogram helps you see the shape. The mean and median in a boxplot help you summarize where the data are centered.

Use both: ask what the distribution looks like, then check whether the numerical summaries tell the same story.

Bivariate visualization

How are two variables related?

Scatterplot

For two numeric variables, the natural starting point is usually a scatterplot. With a scatterplot we can get a sense of the strength and direction of the correlation as well the form, clusters as well as potential outliers.

R

ten_scatter <- ten %>%
  filter(
    !is.na(percent_below),
    !is.na(percent_on_mastered)
  )

ggplot(
  ten_scatter,
  aes(
    x = percent_below,
    y = percent_on_mastered
  )
) +
  geom_point(
    color = "blue"
  )

Python

ten_scatter = ten[
    ten["percent_below"].notna() &
    ten["percent_on_mastered"].notna()
]

plt.figure(figsize=(6, 4))
plt.scatter(
    ten_scatter["percent_below"],
    ten_scatter["percent_on_mastered"],
    c = "blue"
)

Relationships are not always linear

A scatterplot can reveal patterns that a correlation or summary statistic can hide:

  • curves
  • thresholds
  • clusters
  • influential observations
  • changing spread

Tip

Plot first, model second. If you plan to use a linear model, inspect whether a linear relationship looks plausible.

Adding a fitted line

Can help summarize the direction of a relationship, but not an evidence of causality.

x = ten_scatter["percent_below"]

When color helps—and when it doesn’t

Color can make groups easier to distinguish, but it can also create clutter.

You need only look at the last two plots to realize this

Use a legend when the group identity matters. Remove redundant encodings when it does not or when it does not add visual distinction.

Tip

Each visual variable should have a clear job. Do not map variables to aesthetics just because an option exists.

Alternative: Opt for faceting!

Faceting

Faceting shows the same basic plot separately for groups. This can make comparisons easier because the visual structure is repeated but clutter is avoided.

Faceting is your friend when

  • groups answer the same question;
  • a common axis makes comparison easier;
  • separate legends would become cumbersome.

In ggplot2 you have two options:

  • facet_wrap(): splits data based on one grouping variable into separate panels and wraps them across rows/columns to fit the available space
  • facet_grid(): organizes panels to define a structured row × column grid, typically using two grouping variables

In matplotlib you have to implement the underlying logic to group or arrange subplots within a grid explicitly.

Faceting in R


ten_facet <- ten %>%

Layering in R

Modern graphics systems let us layer multiple summaries in the same coordinate system. For example: distribution + observations + summary

Layer order matters: later layers are drawn on top.

load("data/PluckData.RData")

x_axis <- scale_x_continuous(
    name = "Letter Grade",
    limits = c(0.00, 4.00), breaks = round(
      seq(0.333, 4.00, 1/3), 3
      # sequence and round to get appropriate breaks
    ),
    labels = c("F", "D-", "D", "D+", "C-", "C", "C+", "B-", "B", "B+", "A-", "A")
  )

# theme customizes the non-data components of your plot
my_theme <- theme(
    axis.text = element_text(size = 16),
    # axis.text.x = element_text(angle = 45, hjust = 1),
    axis.title = element_text(size = 18),
    plot.title = element_text(
    size = 20,
    hjust = 0.50
    )
  )

# Create histogram as *first* layer
ggplot(data = dataset, aes(x = GPA)) +
  geom_histogram( # first layer
    color = "black", fill = "lightgrey", bins = 10 
  )+
  geom_boxplot( # second layer
    color = "black", fill = "lightgrey",
    aes(y = -4), width = 3
  )+ 
  geom_point( # third layer
    aes(y = -4), color = "black" # notice we used `color` rather than `fill`
  ) + x_axis + my_theme +
  labs(y = "Frequency", title = "Letter Grade Distribution")

Here, we build the visualization in layers:

  • geom_histogram() shows the overall shape and frequency of GPA values.
  • geom_boxplot() adds a compact summary of the median, quartiles, and potential outliers.
  • geom_point() shows the individual observations behind those summaries.

Because each layer represents the same variable (GPA) in a different way, combining them lets us see the overall distribution, the summary numbers and the observations that produced it.

The order of the geom_*() functions matters: later layers are drawn on top of earlier layers. If the dots had to appear behind the boxplot, how will you change the code?

About aes(y = -4)

The negative positioning of the box-plot (and the scatterplot) on the y axis ensures that both appear underneath the histogram.

About scale_x_continuous()

The scale_x_continuous() code is not part of the layering itself. Here it is used to customize the x-axis by first segmenting the GPA scale with desired breaks and then translating numeric GPA values into familiar letter-grade labels displayed as F, D-, D, …, A.

Layering in Matplotlib

dataset = pd.read_csv("data/pluckclean_2.csv")

Visualization often requires data transformation

Good visualization often depends on good data structure.

Sometimes the data are not organized in a convenient form for the plot you want. Suppose we want to compare the distributions of two variables:

Extraversion     Neurotocism
     17               11
     19               15
     14               12
     21               18
     ...              ...

The data are in wide form: each variable has its own column. But we want to use one plotting specification for both distributions, with the variable name determining the color.

For this we need the variables to be in long form: one column identifies the variable, and another contains its values.

Value        Variable

17           Extraversion
11           Neurotocism
19           Extraversion
15           Neurotocism
14           Extraversion
12           Neurotocism
      ...

The key idea

Sometimes we reshape the data not because the data are wrong, but because a different structure makes the visualization easier to specify.

Long format: the idea

Suppose we want to compare the distributions of two achievement measures and use color to distinguish which measure each observation belongs to.

In our original dataframe, the two measures are stored in separate columns. To map the type of achievement measure to color using one plotting specification, it is useful to reshape the data from wide to long form.

R

ten_long <- ten %>%
  pivot_longer(
    cols = c(
      percent_below,
      percent_on_mastered
    ),
    names_to = "measure",
    values_to = "value"
  ) %>%
  select(
    district_name,
    school_name,
    measure,
    value

Principles of Graphical Integrity

Edward Tufte (Reading)

Tufte: graphical excellence

Edward Tufte, a pioneering figure in Data Visualization, lays out 6 principles of graphical excellence.

Read this blog that delves into the details of his principles through a review of his seminal work: The Visual Display of Quantitative Information

Tufte’s concern is not whether a chart is “pretty”. It is whether the graphic communicates quantitative information clearly, precisely, and efficiently.

A strong graphic should:

  • make the important information easy to see;
  • support comparison;
  • reveal useful detail;
  • avoid unnecessary visual burden;
  • most importantly never mislead!

Tufte: show the data

The graphic should make the data the subject of attention.

Ask:

What in this figure is actually carrying quantitative information?

Everything else should justify its presence by helping the reader understand that information.

Tufte: data-ink

Data-ink is the part of a graphic that represents the data. Use it wisely!

A practical question to ask each time:

If I remove a certain element of my graphic, do I lose information?

Potential non-data-ink includes:

  • decorative backgrounds
  • redundant borders
  • excessive gridlines
  • unnecessary 3-D effects
  • repeated labels

Note

“Remove chartjunk” does not mean “remove everything that is not data.” Supporting elements can improve reading. The test is whether they help the quantitative message.

Tufte: graphical integrity

A graphic should represent numerical relationships truthfully.

Watch for:

  • misleading axis limits
  • inconsistent scales
  • distorted area or volume encodings
  • missing baselines or context
  • decorative perspective that changes apparent magnitude

The visual effect should not exaggerate the numerical effect.

The lie factor

Tufte’s “lie factor” compares the scale/magnitude of the apparent effect shown in the graphic with the effect in the data.

[ = {} ]

A value near 1 represents faithful graphical representation.

Tip

For bar charts, a zero baseline is generally important because bar length/area encodes magnitude directly. But this isn’t a universal rule, just more adhering to Tufte’s principles.

A classic case of distortion: Truncated Axes

Suppose two values are 95 and 100.

A bar chart starting at zero shows a small difference.

A chart starting at 90 can make the same difference look enormous.

Warning

Ask: Is the visual design amplifying the difference beyond what the numbers justify?

Data density and comparison

Good graphics can communicate many values in a small space while preserving readability.

Tufte emphasizes:

  • comparisons across observations;
  • multiple levels of detail;
  • context;
  • information density without distortion.

This is why small multiples can be powerful: repeated structure allows the eye to compare panels efficiently.

Our Tennessee education dataset contains achievement information across districts, schools, subjects, and student subgroups. We had already conducted some wrangling to better understand the data.

What more can be done?

  1. Examine individual variables. What does the distribution of percent_below look like across districts? Does any observation look out of place?
  2. Compare different groups. How does percent_below differ between ELA and Math? How does it vary across district_size categories?
  3. Examine change over time. What does the distribution of change_below look like? For this we may need data across several years
  4. Examine a relationship between two variables. Is district size related to achievement outcomes?
  5. Look for patterns you might otherwise miss. Are there particular districts, subjects, or groups that stand out?

Takeaways

  1. EDA is a way of thinking, not a list of plots.
  2. Start with the question and the data.
  3. Choose visualizations from the variables and comparison you need to make.
  4. Use Tufte’s principles to protect truthfulness, clarity, and efficiency.
  5. Use the Grammar of Graphics as a way to think about data, mappings, marks, and layout.
  6. Treat visualization as part of an iterative analytical process.
